The Preamp: Building Blocks

One amplifier card in 1968, and twenty years of input channels built from the same parts: the M124, the M600, the M441, the M460 and its plug-in boards, the M462 that shares its filters, the M470, the M490 and the M520.

Timeline

  • 1968|1M124 audio amplifier. 60-5 input transformer, gain strapped at the edge connector, active balanced output, 48 V. 'An entire console system may be designed around the M124 plug-in card.'
  • 1970|3M600 universal amplifier: 60-4 input and 70-3 output transformers, SA122 amplifier assembly.

  • 1977|10M470A input module: the same chain on one board, with an insert for the M472 equalizer.

Script and slides

2.2.1 Cold open: one card

VO In 1968 Ward-Beck printed a sales sheet for one amplifier card, the M124. The last line makes a big promise. 'An entire console system, monitor amplifiers excepted, may be designed around the M124 plug-in card.' That sounds like a sales line. It was the plan. This episode follows the preamp, the first amplifier a microphone meets, and shows how Ward-Beck kept building new consoles out of the same few blocks for twenty years.

2.2.2 The M124: one card, any job

VO The M124 is a printed circuit card three and a half by five and a half inches. A specially designed input transformer, the type 60-5, sits on the card, so the same input could take a 37.5, 150 or 600 ohm source. Gain runs from 45 to 60 decibels, set by strapping on the edge connector, not inside the card. So any M124 could replace any other, 'without regard to function.' The output was actively balanced, with no output transformer, good for a watt into the line. It ran on 48 volts. Two drawings show the same card wired once as a microphone input and once as a line input.

2.2.3 The transistors that stayed

VO Read the M124 parts list and four transistor types do most of the work: the 2N3569, the 2N3645, the 2N2102 and the 2N4037. Two years later the M600 universal amplifier was built around its own amplifier assembly, and the same types appear on it again. The M600 went the other way on the output: a 60-4 transformer in, a 70-3 transformer out, and gain from 50 to 70 decibels set by one resistor. What matters here is that by 1970 Ward-Beck had an amplifier it trusted, and it kept that recipe.

2.2.4 The M441: a whole channel on a card

VO The M124 was an amplifier. The M441 was a channel. The M441A schematic is dated November 1970. A sensitivity switch and a phase switch feed a 60-9 input transformer. Then something new for Ward-Beck: an integrated circuit as the preamplifier. But the output stage still uses the 2N2102 and 2N4037 pair, one half inverting, one half not, for an actively balanced output. The booster after the fader lives on its own drawing, board 182. The preamp is board 181. On the M441M of 1973, a Penny and Giles fader sits between the two, and the booster adds back 15 decibels.

2.2.5 Job 73050: old and new in one desk

VO In September 1973 the T1202 console, job 73050, shows both generations side by side. Every input is an M441M channel amplifier, feeding the mix buses at minus 10. After the two master faders sit two M124 cards, wired as program amplifiers on 48 volts, putting out plus 14. The card from 1968 had not been retired. It had found a new job. That's what 'without regard to function' meant in practice.

2.2.6 The 460: three boards and a motherboard

VO In 1974 the input module changes shape. The M460 card is 41 millimetres wide and 360 tall, and it's a carrier. Plugged onto its motherboard are separate boards, each with its own drawing number. The 4-110 is the preamp and high-pass filter, drawn in March 1974: five discrete transistors working as an operational amplifier, 30 decibels of sensitivity range, and a 12 decibel per octave high-pass that sweeps up to 250 hertz. The 4-126 is the equalizer filter board. And the 8-109 is the amplifier. The 4-110's own description says it was designed for the M460 and the M461 alike.

2.2.7 The 8-109: one amplifier everywhere

VO The 8-109 is a small plug-in amplifier with an actively balanced output good for plus 30. It came in lettered versions, A through G, the gain changed by a few parts. Look at its parts list: 2N3569, 2N3645, 2N2102, 2N4037. The M124's transistors, six years later. Gerry Bell drew it. The archive finds it in the M460, M461 and M462, the M463 submasters, the M464 filter, the M465, the M466 and M467 dynamics cards, the M471, and the M497 masters. The dynamics cards carry the same booster.

2.2.8 Same filters: the M460 and the M462

VO In 1974 Ward-Beck also sold the M462, an equalizer in its own short module. Open its manual next to the M460M manual and the circuit descriptions match almost word for word. The preamp output feeds 'the first of four cascaded summing amplifiers', which mix the signal with correction signals 'derived from the outputs of the 4-126 filter networks.' Same board, same drawing number. Same bands: highs from 1.6 to 25 kilohertz, lows from 25 to 400 hertz, 15 decibels either way. Same 8-109 on the output. The M462 is the M460's equalizer, unplugged from the channel.

2.2.9 One filter board, many modules

VO The 4-126 shows up in more places than the EQ module. The archive finds it in the M460, M460A, B, L and M, in the M462 and M462A, and in the M463 submasters. When a four-band version was wanted, the M462B, the recipe stayed and only the filter board changed: the 4-112, with two mid bands in place of one, feeding the same four summing amplifiers and the same 8-109. The M462 manual says the module was designed for consoles using the M460, M470 and M490 families. Its 500-series twin, the M562, is electrically identical. Only the panel colour and knobs differ.

2.2.10 Inside the M470A

VO The M470A is a complete input channel except for two things: the fader and an optional external equalizer. Interlocked buttons choose one line or two microphones, and a fourth adds a 20 decibel pad. Every input is transformer coupled. The line position bridges the source at 30 thousand ohms. Five transistors form the preamplifier, with a sensitivity control covering 30 decibels. The signal then leaves the card and comes back, an insert point for the M472 equalizer, before a 12 decibel per octave high-pass at 70, 100 or 150 hertz. An actively balanced output stage feeds the phase switch and a balanced insert. A comparator lights a peak lamp at a level set from outside the module. After the fader, a 15 decibel booster feeds a direct output and, through 30 thousand ohm current sources, the master, submaster, solo and four auxiliary buses. Q10 and Q11 drop the 25 volt supply to a quiet 20.

2.2.11 The 470: the same recipe on one board

VO In 1977 the M470A folds the blocks back onto a single board. The order of the chain doesn't change. Input transformer. A preamplifier of five transistors, Q1 through Q5, with a 30 decibel sensitivity range. An insert point for the M472 equalizer. A 12 decibel per octave high-pass, now on pushbuttons: 70, 100 or 150 hertz. Then the fader, and a booster at 15 decibels, which the manual says 'recovers the loss incurred with the fader placed in its nominal 15 dB in-hand position.' The outputs are current sources feeding summing buses. Same 360 millimetre card.

2.2.12 The 490: the fader moves onto the card

VO A year later the M490A mic input brings the fader inside the module: a Penny and Giles slide attenuator on the card itself. The preamp is seven transistors and an IC, with a 30 decibel sensitivity trim beside the input buttons. Channel on and off is not a relay or a switch contact in the audio. It's a CMOS analog switch with more than 100 decibels of isolation, held by a CMOS flip-flop that also takes remote commands. Two inserts in series let a mono channel carry a left and a right processor in the R1200 and R2000 consoles. Same card size again.

2.2.13 The iron: the 60 family

VO Through all of this the first part a microphone met stayed a Ward-Beck transformer. The type numbers tell the story: 60-5 in the M124, 60-4 in the M600, 60-9 in the M441M, 60-11 in the M460M. Then the 60-12, which the archive finds in the M470A, the later M490s and the M520A. The manuals keep the same rule: the microphone input looks 'substantially higher' than the 150 ohm source, and the line input bridges at 20 or 30 thousand ohms. 2.2.14 The 500s: subassemblies come back

VO In the eighties the plug-in boards return, now with SA14 numbers. The M520A mono input carries the SA14-081 preamplifier subassembly: the low-noise preamp, the switched high-pass, and a phantom power button. Its sensitivity control still covers 30 decibels. Its output still goes to an equalizer insert, this time for the M522, 'located above and inline with the input module.' The SA14-077 control assembly is shared by the M520, the M521 and the M523. The SA14-075 stereo preamp serves the M521 and the M471. And the manual calls the card 'a standard WBS module size': 1.60 inches by 14.15 by 6.50. The M460A's size, twelve years on.

2.2.15 An hour on the bench: M462A to M522

VO One page in the archive proves the blocks really were blocks. It's a note on converting an old M462A equalizer into an M522. Step one: 'Remove the 8-109A output amplifier board. No longer used.' Cut two traces, add three jumpers, change four resistors, swap the LM301 op-amps for LM201s if you're rebuilding. About an hour. The note says the modified M462A 'performs as well as the newer 522 equalizer, with the added advantage of adjustable, rather than fixed, Q.' Then relabel it. 2.2.16 Close: the recipe

VO Put the drawings in a row and the recipe barely moves in twenty years. A Ward-Beck transformer. A preamp with about 30 decibels of range. A 12 decibel per octave high-pass. An insert for an equalizer that might live on the channel or in the module above it. A fader. A 15 decibel booster to win back the fader's loss. An actively balanced output. The switching went solid state, but you can still pull an M460 from a 1976 desk, put it in a wooden box, and use it again. Or rack two M460As side by side behind a new front panel.
