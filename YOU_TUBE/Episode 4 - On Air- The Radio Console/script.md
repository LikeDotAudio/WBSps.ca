### 3.2 A radio console is an intercom that mutes to a bus

**VO:** This episode is built on one idea: a radio console is an intercom that mutes signals to a bus. On an intercom a key closes a crosspoint and a voice reaches a destination. On a radio console the channel ON button does the same thing to the program bus, and in the same moment it has to mute the speakers in the room with the open microphone, light the tally and reset the timer. The CHIN Radio drawings for job 900438 show it clearly: alongside the audio functional drawing there's a separate DC functional drawing, covering only tally, timer reset and mute. The R2K console manual puts it in one sentence: «Pressing the channel ON button generates timer reset and mute control.»

### 3.3 One Button, Four Jobs

**VO:** This is the whole idea in a single button. On an R2K console input module, the channel ON button sends the signal to the four stereo program buses, the two mono mix buses, and the channel's direct output. Pressing it does more than pass audio. In the manual's words, it generates timer reset and mute control. The timer reset bus runs to the console's timer electronics, and any module can be jumpered to reset it. The mute control can be jumpered to mute bus A, B or C, and those buses can silence the control room and studio monitor outputs and the cue speaker. Four programmable control outputs give channel on and channel off tallies, or event pulses for outside equipment. The earlier Renaissance drawings carry the same signals: a tally for channel on, a mute bus, and a timer reset bus. One press puts the channel on the program bus, mutes the speakers, lights the tally and resets the timer.

### 3.4 The mute bus

**VO:** Follow the mute through the drawings. Each Renaissance channel can be jumpered onto mute buses A, B and C, run on +21 volts, and each bus mutes a different room's speakers. When the mute has to come from outside the console, there's an opto-isolator assembly, drawing 14-295, so another device's logic never shares a ground with the audio. The control-room and studio monitor modules receive those mutes. The same idea was already in 1975 in the M495 stereo monitor module from the console years, with its mute assign.

### 3.5 Inside the M495

**VO:** When a microphone opens in a radio studio, the control room speakers have to go quiet. In a Ward-Beck console, the M495 stereo monitor module is where that happens. An interlocked bank of push-buttons picks one of five stereo sources from inside the console, and there is a separate talkback input on the left channel only. A balanced, high impedance preamplifier and a ganged level control feed a dual booster amplifier. Each channel gets two outputs. One comes straight from the amplifier. The other runs through the contacts of a muting relay. That relay can be worked by the mute button, or remotely by a ground closure from the console, and a light glows while muting is on. The archive lists the M495 on jobs for CHIN Radio, KRSR, WLYF, and CHYM. The speakers fall silent on command, and an unmuted feed keeps running.

### 3.6 Inside the M201

**VO:** Behind every mute and every on-air lamp, something has to physically throw a switch. The M201 relay module does that work. Its manual calls it an audio switcher: two identical relay circuits, each one choosing between two balanced inputs and sending one to a balanced output. A fixed loss attenuator can be put into the normally open path, which the manual says suits jobs like part-muting, where a feed is dimmed instead of cut. The control input floats at about nine volts and the relay stays at rest. Pull that input to logic zero, near ground, and the relay energizes. Separate adjustable delays set how fast it pulls in and drops out, and two connector pins can sink about twenty milliamps to drive an external control or an indicator lamp. The archive files it with Ward-Beck's S T Series stereo console. One ground closure, and audio is switched, muted or dimmed, with a lamp to show it.

### 3.7 Inside the M202

**VO:** A console push-button only makes contact for a moment, so something has to remember what was pressed. The M202 logic latch module is that memory. It takes three momentary push-button inputs and latches the one that was pressed, so only one of its three outputs is ever latched at a time. It behaves like a bank of interlocking switches, where pressing one releases the other two. A fourth input has an alternate action latch: press once for on, again for off. Each input has a resistor and capacitor network against switch bounce, followed by a Schmitt trigger. A decoder sends out a short clearing pulse so the old latch drops before the new one sets. Each output transistor can sink current, in the manual's words, for control or tally purposes, and every latch starts in the off state at power up. The archive places it in the S T Series stereo console. The console now has a reliable memory for selection and tally.

### 3.11 Inside the M8200

**VO:** Every on-air voice starts as a tiny signal from a microphone. The M8200 is the transformerless preamplifier that raises it to plant level. Its low noise input is a dual differential stage that strongly rejects common mode signals. In front of it sits a common mode filter that barely touches the microphone signal but heavily attenuates radio frequency pickup. A low pass filter near forty kilohertz suppresses interference from television monitors and lighting systems. Gain is set by an optically coupled, light dependent resistor, so sensitivity can be adjusted remotely over a thirty decibel range without spoiling the low noise. The brochure says that control runs on one shielded pair for a thousand feet or more. The archive puts the M8200 at C F T O in 1988 and Centennial College in 1994, where a drawing shows it next to talkback and cough-off outputs. The result was clean gain, adjustable from wherever the operator sat.

### 3.15 The technology: losing the transformer

**VO:** The company that started by designing its own transformers began the nineties by removing one. The M8200 is described as 'the first of its kind': a transformerless microphone preamp with remote sensitivity control over a single shielded pair up to 1,000 feet. The brochure claims a noise floor within 1 dB of the theoretical limit, high RF immunity and high common-mode rejection, with an actively balanced, low-impedance output. It mounts in the MF-82 frame alongside the M8205 and M8206 distribution amplifiers. In the radio consoles, the R2K manuals are full of DIP-switch settings and mode setups, including 'mono force'. These were one standard console configured per station rather than a new design for each. Renaissance added LED VU metering and four telco sends with telephone conferencing.

### 3.16 Inside the M445

**VO:** Not every Ward-Beck meter sat in a console. The M445 extended range meter monitor was made to check plant audio levels. Docked in its rack frame, it was a confidence monitoring system with its own stereo speakers. Pulled out, it was a portable meter running on batteries. A microcontroller reads a rotary encoder and sets the reference level, which a liquid crystal display shows. To cover sensitivities from minus sixty to plus thirty decibels, the meter switches between three input stages: an instrumentation amplifier, a unity gain stage, and a loss stage. A stereo digital volume control changes level only where the audio crosses zero, which removes the zipper effect. A sum and difference mode checks stereo phase, and a seventy-five hertz filter can be switched in to remove hum. The result was one instrument for the rack and the field, with batteries that charged whenever it was docked.

### 3.17 Inside the M405C

**VO:** Before a feed goes to air, someone has to know how loud it is. The M405C extended range V U meter was a rack-mounted instrument for measuring lines across a wide range, from minus fifty to plus thirty decibels in two decibel steps. Signals come in on matching or bridging jacks and pass through an input transformer to a precision step attenuator. Field effect transistors form a high impedance preamplifier, which keeps tracking error low even when the line is loaded. An output amplifier follows. A switch can insert a sixty hertz notch filter at least thirty decibels deep, to cut out mains hum. The meter is fed through a source resistor that gives it the correct ballistic behaviour, zener diodes protect it from overload, and a second output feeds a headset jack. Operators got a standard, trustworthy reading of any line, and they could listen to it too.
