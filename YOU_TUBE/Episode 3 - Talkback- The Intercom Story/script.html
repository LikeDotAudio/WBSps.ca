Talkback: The Intercom Story

Ward-Beck's first big job was intercom, and so was much of its last decade. Crosspoints, the 7000 series, microCOM and microCOM II, turrets, IFB and Minicom: the matrix that let a director talk into the right ear.

3.1 Cold open: a voice in the right ear

VO A director says 'ready camera two', and only camera two hears it. An anchor hears the producer but the audience doesn't. That's intercom, and before Ward-Beck made famous consoles, intercom was how it paid the rent.

3. 1972: 'intercom systems for its existence'

3.2 1971: the CBC job

VO The CBC was consolidating its French and English facilities in Quebec and needed intercom linking studios, cameras, videotape and telecine. Ward-Beck's bid was solid-state matrices with digital control, so only a few wires had to run between master control and the equipment room. Rodger Beck pointed out that it could test itself. Then Ampex asked whether the same switching could carry program audio: about 100 sources to any of 100 destinations.

3.3 The crosspoint

VO An intercom matrix is a grid of switches, and each switch is a crosspoint. The M712 is the matrix switching board, and the M724 and M121 are the crosspoints. The names come straight from the drawings: 'normally off', 'part mute', 'permanently on'. Even the M757 is in here, a 0-to-24 BCD encoder, because something has to tell the matrix which key was pressed.

3.4 Inside the M712 crosspoint

VO At the heart of the 7000 Series sat the crosspoint matrix, and its building block was the M712. Each card is a twelve-by-one balanced audio matrix: twelve station feeds in, and one mixed bus out to an output amplifier. Ward-Beck's brochure explains how it works. Dual operational amplifiers form a solid-state, balanced, transient-free switch. Each input bridges at thirty thousand ohms, so one source can feed many buses without being loaded down. When off, a crosspoint gives one hundred decibels of attenuation. A unity-gain summing amplifier delivers a balanced six-hundred-ohm output, and a direct input lets matrix groups be cascaded. Each crosspoint is switched by grounding a single control wire, and can be set to normally on, normally off, or a partial mute. The M724 was one of these variants, strapped for partial mute. Any station could reach any other, cleanly and without clicks.

3.5 The 7000 series

VO By 1977 it's a catalogue line: the 7000 series. Two basic plug-ins do everything, an amplifier module and a crosspoint module. Each crosspoint card holds twelve crosspoints, switches without clicks at unity gain with 100 dB of isolation, and closes on a contact to ground. Systems could be 120 by 120 and larger, all in standard MF7 frames wired with telephone-type terminal blocks. The preamp would take a dynamic mic or a carbon telephone element, and the 3-watt amp had a solid-state mute. Keep that mute in mind for Episode 3.

3.6 In and out: the 701, 702 and 703

VO Every station in the system needs a way in and a way out. The M701 is the input preamplifier, the M702 the output amplifier, and the M703 does both, with one drawing that shows how all three are strapped. In the archive the M703 has 139 files. It shows up everywhere, from the Glenn Gould Studio to job 920002.

3.7 Inside the M701

VO In the Ward-Beck 7000 Series intercom, every station's voice starts at the M701 Intercom Preamplifier. It takes the microphone at a director's or crew member's position and lifts it to line level for the crosspoint matrix. Its input could be strapped for a one-hundred-fifty-ohm dynamic microphone, a carbon microphone, or both in parallel. The amplifier could even power one or two carbon microphones by itself. Inside, a field-effect transistor works as a variable resistance on the first amplifier stage. It is steered by a rectified sample of the output, which gives the circuit what the manual calls its compressor action, over a range of twenty-five decibels. A front-panel gain control then feeds a power stage with an actively balanced output of up to plus eighteen decibels above one milliwatt. Quiet speakers and loud ones both reached the matrix at a steady, usable level.

3.8 Talkback in the console

VO Intercom moved into the consoles too. The M494 remote line talkback module has controls for air, assign, line and talk. The M529 communication module put talkback inside the ST consoles of the eighties.

37. M494 remote line talkback

3.9 Inside the M494

VO For remote broadcasts, the M494 Remote Line Talkback and Cue Module connected the console to the outside world. It works with an M451 Talkback Module and an M453A Cue Amplifier, and handles up to eight two-way remote lines. Each line is picked with interlocked pushbuttons and terminated at a balanced, centre-tapped six hundred ohms. In listen mode, the selected line passes through relay contacts to the cue amplifier. In cue mode, console program or an off-air feed goes back down the line, and a green light shows it. In talkback mode, a relay connects the talkback amplifier to the line, lights a yellow lamp, and disables cue and listen. Once the line's channel is switched on at its M490 input module, an on-air lockout disables all of these and terminates the line, shown by a red light. The remote crew got cues and instructions, and a line on air was protected.

3.10 microCOM: the matrix gets a brain

VO In 1981 the crosspoints get a microprocessor. MicroCOM gives every station access to every other station, and the crosspoint assignments can be reprogrammed show by show. The ad lists ABC Hollywood, the CBC, England and WLS. In London, Molinare's production complex had digital intercom terminals throughout, running Ward-Beck's custom software. Intercom: the only Ward-Beck product designed so people could argue more efficiently.

48. microCOM ad49. Cross-fade: drawing to module microCOM: from the drawing to the module

3.11 Inside microCOM

VO With microCOM, Ward-Beck rebuilt the intercom around the microprocessor. The central matrix is made of blocks of twenty-four balanced inputs and twenty-four outputs. Each M724B matrix amplifier holds twenty-four solid-state crosspoints feeding one output, so any or all inputs can be mixed onto a bus. Buffer amplifiers isolate the incoming sources. A latch module refreshes small memory capacitors that hold each crosspoint on or off, sweeping all twenty-four buses about every one hundred fifty milliseconds. Controllers take key presses from the stations over a balanced serial data line, so each station needs only three pairs of wires: input, output and control. Any output controller can act as master, so one failure affects only its own group. After a power cut, the system tests itself and relearns its assignments from the terminals. The archive ties microCOM to the Molinare project and to NTC Hollywood.

3.12 microCOM II: configured from a PC

VO MicroCOM II, 1988, is set up from a PC with matrix, terminal, group, block and IFB files written and then sent to the system. The central matrix is built from blocks of 24 inputs by 24 outputs and grows to 960 by 960, and you only install the crosspoints you actually use. It tests itself as well. An M7890 terminal injects tone and listens for it, an M777A watches every crosspoint on a bus, and an M7720 scans every power supply and sounds an alarm when one drifts. TCN-9 in Sydney and CBC Edmonton bought one.

3.13 Inside microCOM II

VO MicroCOM II, documented in 1988, brought the personal computer into the intercom. Its central matrix is still built from twenty-four by twenty-four blocks, which can be joined into matrices as large as nine hundred sixty by nine hundred sixty. All audio is actively balanced at plus eight decibels. Each M724B crosspoint module carries twenty-four balanced field-effect transistor switches, with more than one hundred decibels of cutoff. Microcontrollers refresh the crosspoint data about twenty times a second. A special frame gives each output bus its own program feed for interrupted foldback, so talent hears program until someone talks to them. Assignments are stored in erasable memory, so the system restarts in seconds. An IBM-type personal computer can reprogram any terminal from one central point. Stations could be reassigned without touching hardware. It went to TCN-9 Sydney and to CBC Edmonton.

3.14 IFB and the turrets

VO IFB is the mix-minus feed in the talent's ear: the whole program except their own voice, with the producer able to break in. Through the nineties the CBC kept ordering it: an EHQ custom IFB matrix in 1996, and matrix turrets for Rankin Inlet in 1999. Turrets are small panels of intercom keys for announcers, hosts and guests, and they bring us to radio.
