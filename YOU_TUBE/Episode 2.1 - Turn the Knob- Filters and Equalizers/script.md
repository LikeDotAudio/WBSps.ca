### 2.1.1 Cold open: something to crow about

**VO:** Spring 1969. A Ward-Beck ad in Broadcasting and Communications shows a rooster and a console. The headline says 'Something to crow about!' The customer was Pathé-Humphries of Canada, and the console was the M1006. The ad lists what was inside: eighteen M402 effects equalizers, four M404 hi-lo equalizers, two M406A film compressors. Eighteen equalizers in one desk, from a company that was not yet two years old. This episode is about those equalizers and filters, and about 1974, when Ward-Beck's equalizer knobs stopped clicking and started to turn.

### 2.1.2 Inside the M404

**VO:** The M404 has three active sections: a low-noise preamplifier fed through a shielded input transformer, the filter networks, and an integrated-circuit output amplifier with an actively balanced output. The filters sit between them at unity gain, and a switch can take them out so the card works as a flat amplifier. Each filter is built in two parts. A simple resistor and capacitor network gives the first 6 decibels per octave. Then a pair of field-effect transistors forms a high-impedance isolation amplifier, and positive feedback around it adds another 12 decibels per octave, for an 18 decibel per octave cut. Only resistors are switched to change the frequency. A diode protects the card if the 48 volt supply is connected backwards.

### 2.1.4 Set it up, then punch it in

**VO:** Every control was a switch. Three stacks of concentric knobs gave high and low boost or cut, a presence boost at five frequencies from 700 hertz to 5 kilohertz, a mid-low platform, and a 60 hertz notch. Ward-Beck called the switches transient-free, and the manual explains how they were meant to be used. With the equalizer switched OUT, the operator sets up the curve while the program stays flat. Then, 'at the prescribed time,' the selector goes to IN and the pre-set response modifies the program. That is what an effects equalizer was: a sound you prepare, then cut to.

### 2.1.5 The M404 hi-lo pass equalizer

**VO:** Its partner was the M404, the active hi-lo pass equalizer, also 1968. Same case, same panel cutout, same plug. One knob picks a low cut from 70 hertz to 5 kilohertz. Another picks a high cut from 500 hertz to 12 kilohertz. Both roll off at 18 decibels per octave. A third knob chooses HI, LO, or both, and with both in, the manual says, you get a pass-band 'such as telephone effect.' While one filter is on air, the other can be set up for the next cue.

### 2.1.7 A and B: the rework

**VO:** In January 1970 a draftsman with the initials DJG drew new plug connections for the 'M402A and M404A.' The A versions used a 22-pin connector and a panel status lamp, and the data sheet describes a 'unique solid-state audio switch' to insert the equalizer. The M404A outline drawing, February 1970, notes the cases were designed for side-by-side mounting. Then came the M402B, its schematic redrawn in 1971 by PHL. The B dropped the 60 hertz notch and the mid-low platform. It added presence cut as well as boost, and a high-pass filter to remove low frequency noise. Its brochure gives a second address: Ward-Beck Systems Inc., Larkin Street, Buffalo.

### 2.1.8 Steps, not sweeps

**VO:** All of these were stepped: five presence frequencies, ten or eleven cut-off points, two decibels a click. Steps are precise and repeatable, and an operator could reset a show from notes. But if the problem sat at 3 kilohertz and the switch offered 2.5 or 3.4, you lived with it. In the May 1975 US price list the M402B and the M404A still sell for 415 dollars each.

### 2.1.9 1974: the knobs start to turn

**VO:** In July 1974 a draftsman with the initials DM drew the 4-126 equalizer filter board, the heart of the M462 equalizer. Episode two looked inside it. What matters here is the front panel. Every band has three controls, and none of them click: a frequency knob that sweeps continuously, a Q knob that sets how narrow the bell is, and a boost-cut knob worth 15 decibels either way. A pushbutton turns the high and low bands from peaking to shelving. That is what we now call a parametric equalizer: you choose the frequency, the width and the amount.

### 2.1.10 Three levels of equalizer

**VO:** Ward-Beck offered it at three levels. The M462A was the simplest: shelving lows and highs, and a sweeping mid with variable Q, 'ideal for use in recording and production audio consoles in all but the most sophisticated systems.' The M462 put variable Q and peaking or shelving on all three bands. The M462B went to four bands, splitting the middle into two overlapping sections that reach from 100 hertz to 12.8 kilohertz. Its component layout is dated September 1974. A later Ward-Beck brochure says the B gives 'sophistication and flexibility not attainable from any other console channel equalizer at the marketplace,' with curves that 'exceed those of most graphic equalizers.'

### 2.1.11 One filter, many modules

**VO:** The 4-126 board did not stay in one module. ### 2.1.12 Inside the M464

**VO:** In the M464 the signal enters a preamplifier trimmed for unity gain, then meets the low frequency cut. An isolation amplifier feeds the first section of the high-pass filter, worth 6 decibels per octave, and a second, 12 decibel section follows, for 18 in all. A rotary switch picks one of eleven corner frequencies. The low pass filter is built the same way, 6 plus 12, with its own eleven corners from 500 hertz to 15 kilohertz. Two insert switches, each with an LED, put either filter in the path in any combination. A separate output amplifier card gives an actively balanced output, and transistors in the supply decoupling limit the current if a part fails.

### 2.1.13 The M464: 18 decibels per octave

**VO:** The filter got its new card too. The M464 high-pass low-pass filter has a schematic dated November 1976 and a front panel drawn in February 1977. It keeps the 1968 slope, 18 decibels per octave, with eleven switched corner frequencies on each side: from 27 hertz for the low cut, and from 500 hertz to 15 kilohertz for the high cut. Each section now has its own insert pushbutton with an LED. Ward-Beck listed the uses plainly: simulating 'limited frequency program paths' such as telephone circuits, and rolling off rumble, hum or tape hiss.

### 2.1.14 The M472: a sweep in every channel

**VO:** In 1977 the L-series consoles needed an equalizer in every input. The M472 schematic is dated June 1977, drawn by MRW, and the functional diagram carries the engineering initials RKB. [ARCHIVIST: confirm RKB is Rodger K. Beck.] It is a smaller card than the M462: a high shelf with a fixed knee at 10 kilohertz, a peaking mid that sweeps from 400 to 6,400 hertz with a HIGH-LOW Q button, and a low shelf whose knee sweeps from 50 to 200 hertz. It plugs into the insert point of the M470 input module. The L2042 specification says each of the twenty inputs 'may be fitted' with one, and that it can be left out 'for reasons of economy.'

### 2.1.15 Where they went

**VO:** The job records show where the 1970s equalizers went. CFTO's job 76045 used the M462, M462A and M464. The L2042, job 77012, and the L3242, job 77038, carried M472s, with M462s alongside. The Maryland Center for Public Broadcasting's L3242A, project 790236, used the M462, M462B, M464 and M472, and one of its drawings puts an M464 in the effects switcher. The cards kept working long after: TVOntario's M1204B in 1988 and CHIN Radio in 1990 both list the M462A.

### 2.1.16 Ward-Beck's word for it

**VO:** Ward-Beck rarely said 'parametric.' Its manuals and brochures say 'state variable equalizer,' after the filter circuit inside, and the LS series brochure calls them 'SVE equalizers.' The word does appear in the 1984 brochure for the ST2442 and ST3642 consoles: the M462 series is offered 'up to comprehensive four band parametric unit, featuring continuous resolution control of frequency, bandwidth, and amplitude.' By then the design was ten years old.

### 2.1.17 Close: after the seventies

**VO:** The family kept going. By 1984 the M522 was the channel equalizer, with a button for Q instead of a knob. A note in the archive explains how to convert an older M462A into an M522, and says the converted card has 'the added advantage of adjustable, rather than fixed, Q control.' [ARCHIVIST: who wrote the M462A to M522 conversion note, and when.] Stereo versions followed: the M462C and M562C in 1985, the M472A in 1986, and the M922 in 1994. But the idea of a filter you could tune by ear, live, on a plug-in card, goes back to a board drawn in July 1974.
