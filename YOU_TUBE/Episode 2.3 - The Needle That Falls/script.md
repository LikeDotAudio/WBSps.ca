## Script and slides

### 2.3.1 Cold open: the needle that falls

**VO:** Look at the meter, not the knobs. On most consoles a needle jumps up when the sound gets louder. On this one it rests at zero and falls. Every decibel it drops is a decibel the compressor next to it has taken away. This episode is about that needle: Ward-Beck's compressors, the M406 and the M466, the de-esser inside them, and the narrow meter module that showed the operator what they were doing.

### 2.3.2 Why broadcasters squeeze

**VO:** A live voice covers a huge range, from a whisper to a shout. A transmitter, a tape machine or a phone line handles much less. Push the peaks too hard and the transmitter overmodulates. Let the quiet parts drop and they disappear under the road noise in a listener's car. Ward-Beck's first manual explained it in two sentences. A compressor reduces the dynamic range of the program. A limiter prevents overload distortion from excessive peaks. The M406 could be set up to do either job.

### 2.3.4 A compressor the size of a fader

**VO:** Rodger Beck wrote a paper on it, 'A Unique Compressor Limiter'. His conclusion lists what mattered: fully balanced, five compression ratios, low distortion, and it 'occupies approximately the space allocated for a conventional straight line mixing attenuator'. A whole compressor in the space of one fader. The M406B followed in December 1969, with three stacks of concentric knobs for threshold, compression and recovery. Its gain element, a transistor switched on and off far above the audio band.

### 2.3.5 Threshold, ratio, attack and release

**VO:** Four words explain any compressor. Threshold is where it starts working. Ratio is how hard it pushes back. On the M406B at 3 to 1, a 3 dB rise at the input gives 1 dB more at the output. It offered 2 to 10 to 1, plus a limit position steeper than 20 to 1. Attack is how fast it reacts: 10 milliseconds compressing, 100 microseconds limiting. Release, Ward-Beck's recovery, is how fast it lets go, up to 5 seconds. The manual recommends 2 to 1 for dialogue and 3 or 5 to 1 for 'more erratic music'. And it says to work halfway up the slope, so the unit can pull loud passages down and let quiet ones come back up.

### 2.3.6 The first de-esser

**VO:** Close-miked voices have one stubborn problem: the letter S. Sibilance carries a lot of energy above a few kilohertz, and a compressor set for the whole voice lets it straight through. The M406B handled this with a small knob on top of the recovery control, marked de-ess, from 0 to 8. It added up to 8 dB of extra high-frequency compression. The manual is specific: use it only on the 2 to 1 slope, with a short recovery, to help reduce sibilance in a person's voice.

### 2.3.7 Gain reduction you can see

**VO:** A compressor works out of sight, so it needs a meter. The M406 had a selector on its middle knob, VU or GR. In VU, an external meter showed output level. In GR it read zero when compression began and swung downward, in decibels, as the compressor worked. Ward-Beck sold the meter separately. The United States price schedule for May 1975 lists the M406B at 435 dollars, and on the next line, 'Edgewise gain reduction meter for M406B', at 50 dollars. Edgewise means a meter turned on its side, narrow enough to fit a console strip.

### 2.3.8 1976: the M466

**VO:** By 1976 the console had changed, and so had the compressor. The M466 compressor-limiter is a plug-in card 1.6 inches wide, made for the 460-series frames.The main idea is separation. The compressor and the limiter are two independent circuits, each with its own threshold, release and insert button. The compressor has four slopes, 1.5, 2, 3 and 5 to 1, and up to 16 dB of make-up gain. The limiter is fixed at 50 to 1, with a green light at 6 dB of limiting and a yellow one at 12. The front-panel drawing calls 12 dB an overload. If it stays lit, turn the fader down.

### 2.3.9 How the M466 turns the gain down

**VO:** Inside, the M406's chopper is gone. The M466 uses an electronic attenuator that works as a voltage multiplier, with the audio on one input and a control voltage on the other. Its four transistors are thermally coupled, so the console's heat doesn't change the gain. A plug-in card, the 6-106 compressor control amplifier, drawn in July 1976, shapes that control voltage. Its logarithmic amplifier makes the compression slope a straight line in decibels. The compressor's detector produces a positive voltage, with a 10 millisecond attack. The limiter's detector produces a negative one, with a 50 microsecond attack. Both are summed and drive the same attenuator.

### 2.3.10 De-essing, the M466 way

**VO:** On the M466, the de-esser is a frequency-dependent compressor. Press DS and a filter goes into the compressor's control path, so only frequencies above a chosen point, anywhere from 1 to 6 kilohertz, can push the gain down. The same switch halves the release time. The audio isn't filtered. The whole signal dips for an instant when an S comes through. The manual says to run it like a peak limiter, so gain reduction happens only on high-frequency peaks. Ward-Beck's ST console brochure suggests using these cards as 'anti-sibilance controllers in individual microphone channels'.

### 2.3.11 The vertical meter

**VO:** The M466 has no meter of its own. Its technical data says only 'provision for external VU meter'. The meter lives next door, in the M466M compressor meter of September 1976 or in the M467: a lit edgewise VU meter standing upright, because a standard VU meter is wider than the whole module. The scale runs top to bottom: plus 3, zero VU, minus 5, minus 10, infinity. With no signal, the meter is biased to read zero. As the compressor works, the needle falls. Limiting doesn't move it. That shows on the two lights. Three buttons marked COMP TIE link compressors on one of two buses. The unit with the highest control voltage controls them all, so a stereo pair turns down together and the image doesn't shift.

### 2.3.12 The gate beside the meter

**VO:** Below the M467's meter is the opposite of a compressor: a noise gate. When the signal falls below the threshold, anywhere from 0 to minus 50 dBm, the gate turns the channel down by up to 50 dB, releasing over a tenth of a second to 5 seconds. Its attack is set at the factory to about 1 millisecond. Any faster, the manual warns, and you get 'an audible Fourier click'. Inside is a differential amplifier. A field-effect transistor shorts one input for unity gain, and when it switches off, the two paths cancel. The ST brochure suggests it for interviews, against studio or street noise.

### 2.3.15 Close: the sound of control

**VO:** What did all this do to broadcast sound? It made it steady. A newsreader stays at one level from the first word to the last. A shout doesn't overmodulate the transmitter, and a murmur isn't lost in road noise. Rodger Beck's stated goal was 'indiscernible automatic control of program transmission'. Twenty years later, the 1995 M8207 did the job on a card for the M8200 frame, with a VCA and a modest 2 to 1 ratio. Ward-Beck called it 'virtually transparent', and pitched it at digital workstations. The goal hadn't changed. Only the needle was gone.
