1.3 Two men in a garage

VO April 1967. Ron Ward and Rodger Beck set up shop in a garage in Toronto. The split was clear from day one: Ward handled sales and the money, Beck engineered and designed. Their own brochure put it plainly. Uncompromising quality in audio control consoles and switching systems would be well received by the broadcasting, film and recording industries.

1.4 What do you do when it isn't in the catalogue?

VO That's how Broadcaster magazine opened its 1971 profile. If you're the CBC merging French and English facilities in Quebec and you need intercom between studios, cameras, videotape and telecine, you call Ward-Beck. If you're CKEY in Toronto, moving into studios on top of the new Star building, you call Ward-Beck. Ward said they went after 'the complex jobs', the ones considered headaches by companies built to make multiples of the same item. That one sentence explains the whole company: every client was a custom job. 1971 was also the year Canadian content rules came in for radio, and the same magazine ran a cartoon about it: a lady wearing nothing but maple leafs, and the line 'Actually, she's an American, but we've managed to maintain Canadian content.' The leaves, not the hockey team. The Leafs haven't covered anything since 1967, the year Ward-Beck started. Ward-Beck didn't need the leaves. It was Canadian content all the way through.

1.8 Inside the M402A

VO The M402A was the equalizer in each channel of a Ward-Beck mixing console, mounted on the control panel in line with the channel's straight-line fader, so the operator could reshape a source before it reached the mix. Inside are three active stages: a low-noise transistor preamplifier fed by a shielded input transformer, a unity-gain equalizer section, and an integrated-circuit output amplifier with an actively balanced output. Every curve comes from switched resistor and capacitor networks in operational-amplifier feedback loops: ten decibels of boost at thirty hertz and at fifteen kilohertz, five presence frequencies from seven hundred hertz to five kilohertz, and a field-effect transistor parallel-T filter that notches sixty hertz hum by more than thirty decibels. An in-out pushbutton gave instant comparison, and the transient-free switches could be adjusted while the program ran.

1.9 Inside the M406B

VO Next in the channel came dynamics. The M406B compressor-limiter shared the slim console-panel format of the equalizers and ran from the same forty-eight volt regulated supply, holding program level within bounds. Its gain cell is unusual. A transistor is switched fully on and off at about seven hundred and fifty kilohertz, far above the audio band, and the ratio of on-time to off-time sets the attenuation; a low-pass filter then recovers the audio. A comparator builds that pulse from a sawtooth and a control voltage, made by rectifying the program and storing it on a capacitor, with the recovery rate chosen by switch. A field-effect transistor buffers that voltage and drives the gain-reduction meter. The operator got slopes from two to one through ten to one, a limit position beyond twenty to one, and up to eight decibels of de-essing.

1.10 The 600 series: amplifiers for the whole plant

VO Consoles are one part of a station. Behind the wall are distribution amplifiers, monitor amps and line amps. 1.11 Inside the M600

VO The M600 was the workhorse of the 600 series: a rack plug-in, ten to an MF6 frame, around which, the manual says, "an entire audio system may be designed." The signal enters a shielded input transformer strapped for one hundred fifty or six hundred ohm sources. Three transistors in an NPN and PNP arrangement form a low-noise, wide-range preamplifier, followed by a field-effect transistor stage that rolls off response beyond the audio spectrum. A direct-coupled driver and output section, with a current source that keeps supply ripple out of the signal, ends in paralleled complementary transistors driving the output transformer, while heavy negative feedback holds distortion negligible. One external resistor sets gain anywhere from fifty to seventy decibels, with up to one watt of output.

1.13 The technology: their own iron, and solid-state switching

VO Two technical choices defined early Ward-Beck. First, they designed their own transformers. The type numbers form a family: 60 for input, 70 for output, 80 for power. The 70-1 output and 80-1 power drawings are dated 1966 and checked by R. Beck, which is before the company formally existed. The 60-4 input transformer went into the M600. Second, switching. For the CBC intercom tender, Ward-Beck proposed solid-state matrices with digital control, so only a few wires had to run between master control and the equipment room. Rodger Beck pointed out that the system could test itself, automatically or on command. That was 1971, and Ampex then hired them to design program-quality versions of the same switching.

1.15 The proposal: CHCH-TV, 9 November 1972

VO The best way to hear how Ward-Beck sounded in 1972 is to read one of its proposals. On the ninth of November, Ron Ward wrote to Mr. W. Jeynes, Director of Engineering at CHCH-TV in Hamilton. «Dear Mr. Jeynes. I take pleasure in submitting herewith the Ward-Beck proposal for the supply of an Intercom System and associated control panels and an Audio Control Console designed expressly for sophisticated television production applications.» There's no sales pitch. It's one engineer writing to another, and the authority comes from the company it keeps: «The equipment proposed comprises well proven design currently in operation throughout the world.» Then the list: the CBC, the National Film Board, the National Arts Centre, CBS in New York, WWL-TV in New Orleans. Then the parts, by name: «Penny and Giles conductive plastic technology attenuators, Clare-Pendar sealed, illuminated, alternate-action pushbuttons, Weston and Simpson meters, teflon wire, military-grade rotary selector switches, and the extensive use of tantalum and computer grade capacitors.» All of it to «guarantee superior performance in the Hamilton environment». If you know Hamilton, that was a real engineering requirement. It thinks about the operator too: «Generous knee and leg room exists to maximize operator comfort. In addition, a full-length padded vinyl armrest is provided to minimize operator fatigue.» And it treats the deal with the care of an aerospace contract. Forty-nine thousand, two hundred and fifty dollars. Five weeks for the intercom, eighteen for the console. Twenty percent on approved drawings, seventy at factory acceptance, ten after installation. «Yours very truly, R. W. Ward, President.»
