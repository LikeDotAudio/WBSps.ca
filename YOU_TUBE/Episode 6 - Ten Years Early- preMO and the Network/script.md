### 6.2 On the network: RAVENNA and AES67

**VO:** For fifty years, audio in a broadcast plant travelled on copper: one balanced pair per signal, through jackfields, transformers and distribution amplifiers. Every one of those was Ward-Beck's business. In 2013 the Audio Engineering Society published AES67, a common way to send audio as packets over ordinary Ethernet, drawing on systems like RAVENNA that were already running. One cable can carry hundreds of channels, and any device on the network can listen to any other. The CBC Montreal intercom in 1971 was a matrix of wires. This is the same problem, solved with a switch.

### 6.3 The first network products

**VO:** Ward-Beck's first answer was to do on the network what it had always done on copper: convert, meter and listen. The 32ME-N, from 2014, takes 16 AES signals onto an AES67 network and brings 16 back, with a meter on every one of the 32 channels. The sheet calls it «a bi-directional AES67 converter on steroids». The AMS8-1AN, the same year, pulls eight signals off the network to a row of LED meters and a built-in speaker, so you can see and hear them. The AMS2-N, in 2016, is a one-rack-unit monitor speaker that listens to RAVENNA streams as easily as an AES input. Its headline reads «This monitor rocks! Hear your networked audio». The MLC8-N mixes eight analog or AES sources, sets their levels, and delivers the mix to the network.

### 6.4 preMO-A1: the microphone on the network

**VO:** Then they went to the start of the chain. «The dream of networked microphones made reality», says the preMO-A1 sheet. «Combining 50 years of pre-amplifier and format conversion experience, the preMO-A1 is the convergence of analog audio, digital conversion, and IP based networking.» It's a microphone preamp, an analog-to-digital converter, an AES67 stream builder and a network interface in one aluminium box, designed, the sheet says, with input from trailblazers of broadcast system engineering. You plug a microphone into the XLR, and an Ethernet cable into the back. That one cable carries the audio out and the power in. No snake, no stage box, no copper pair back to the control room.

### 6.5 Inside the preMO-A1

**VO:** The input is a combo jack that takes a microphone or a line, from minus 60 to plus 10 dBm, with 48-volt phantom power for condenser mics. The preamplifier is CMOS controlled, with up to 63 dB of gain set from the knob or remotely. It converts at 48 or 96 kilohertz, 24 bits, and the sheet claims more than 110 dB of dynamic range, credited to an analog-to-digital channel summation technique. The stream leaves through a standard RJ45 jack with low latency. A web page handles everything the old consoles did with jumpers and drawings: status, synchronization, the media clock, the network transport, the session description and who's connected to what. It runs from Power over Ethernet or a small adapter, and the case is built for the twenty-four-seven life of a broadcast plant. Later firmware added NMOS discovery and SMPTE ST 2110-30, so the rest of the plant could find it on its own.

### 6.6 Why the preamp belongs at the microphone

**VO:** A microphone puts out a tiny signal, around a thousandth of a volt. For fifty years that signal travelled down a long copper pair, through a snake or a wall box, to a preamp in a console, picking up hum and losing level along the way. Ward-Beck spent decades building transformers, balanced inputs and careful grounding to protect it. The preMO takes the opposite approach. It raises the level and converts it to digital within a metre of the microphone, and from there the audio travels as numbers, which don't pick up hum and don't lose level over distance. The microphone is one more device on the network, with an address, a clock and a name.

### 6.7 preMO-221 and preMO-222

**VO:** In 2021 the preMO became a pair. The preMO-221 and 222 each put two microphone preamps on the network, each with its own mute, through XLR or quarter-inch inputs. The 221 adds a stereo headset amplifier and a balanced stereo line output on pluggable terminal blocks. The 222 drives two headsets, one jack on the front and one on the back, each with its own volume control. Both are AES67 and AES70 compliant, and both speak SMPTE 2110. There's a GPIO option on two RJ45 jacks, with illuminated capacitive touch switches, red and green or red and blue, so a talent can key a mic without a click or a pop. Microphone, headset and talkback in one box on one cable. It's the commentary position, rebuilt for the network.

### 6.8 From turret to touch switch

**VO:** Look at the GPIO option again and you can see the whole history of the company in it. In 1971 the CBC job was intercom: who can talk to whom. In the eighties and nineties it was turrets, small panels of keys for announcers to talk back, cough and switch their mics on. On the preMO the same keys come back as two lit touch switches on a network cable. The talent presses one, the preMO sends that press across the network, and the plant decides what it means. The intercom, the turret and the mic preamp, which used to be three products in three racks, are one small box.

### 6.10 The technology: from copper to packets

**VO:** Look at what the cards do now. The openGear D6201B is a dual 1×4 AES/EBU distribution amplifier with sample-rate conversion. The D6203A and D6204A convert between analog and AES. The D6207A decodes Dolby E and AC-3. The -N products put audio on the network using RAVENNA and AES67, while the 32ME packs 32 channels of metering into one rack unit. The RTO-144 sheets record how every module connects. The jobs have stayed the same: distribute the signal, measure it, route it. The wiring changed from transformers and copper pairs to packets.

### 6.11 Ten years early

**VO:** Put the dates side by side. AES67 in 2013. Ward-Beck's first network converter and meter in 2014. A network microphone preamp, powered by the cable that carries its audio, on the NAB floor in 2017, the same year SMPTE published ST 2110. In 2017 most broadcast plants still ran on copper, and an audio network was something you heard about at a conference. Over the next ten years that changed. New facilities were built around the network, and remote production became normal: a microphone at the venue, the audio on the network, and the mix done hundreds of kilometres away. That's what a preMO did, ten years earlier. It's the same pattern as 1971: a client with a problem nobody's catalogue could solve, and a small Toronto company that built the answer first.

### 6.12 Legacy: built like tanks

**VO:** The last products in the archive are dated 2021: the AMS222 monitor and the preMO-221 and 222 preamps, all of them on the network. In January 2021, after more than fifty years, Ward-Beck announced that it would cease manufacturing. The gear didn't stop. Engineers had always said Ward-Beck was built like tanks, and the consoles and modules are still sought after by engineers and collectors. They've been modelled in software and kept running by the people who own them. A preservation society has gathered the schematics, the manuals and the history, which is how this series exists.
