2.4 Inside the M450

VO Every mix has to land somewhere, and in a Ward-Beck console that place was often the M450. It sits where the channels meet on the mixing bus, or wherever a line needs gain. Two halves of an integrated circuit, backed by power booster transistors, form a balanced summing input that sits virtually at ground, so many fifteen kilohm mixing resistors can feed it without interacting. As a summing amplifier it runs at unity gain; as a program amplifier, twenty-four to forty decibels, set by strapping at the connector, not inside the card. A twelve decibel per octave low-pass filter trims everything above the audio band, and an actively balanced output delivers up to plus thirty decibels above a milliwatt into six hundred ohms. Any M450 could replace any other. The archive places it in CFTO's nineteen seventy-six console and Maryland.

2.6 Inside the M460A

VO The M460A is where a microphone or line first enters the console, and where it is shaped before joining the mix. An input selector chooses microphone, microphone through a twenty decibel pad, or a thirty thousand ohm line input, all transformer coupled. A low-noise preamplifier with thirty decibels of sensitivity range follows, then a twelve decibel per octave high-pass filter, adjustable from twenty-five to two hundred fifty hertz. The equalizer gives plus or minus fifteen decibels in three bands: high shelving from one point six to twenty-five kilohertz, a mid peaking band with adjustable Q, and lows from twenty-five to four hundred hertz. It adds in-phase or out-of-phase correction signals to the original, which the manual credits for its low noise and distortion. An insert point, phase reversal, fader, booster, pan and sends complete the channel.

2.7 Inside the M462

VO Not every channel carried its own tone controls, so Ward-Beck built the M462 equalizer as a module that could be switched into a signal path. A preamplifier sets overall gain to unity, then four cascaded summing amplifiers mix the original signal with correction signals from three band-pass filters, each fed in or out of phase by a cut and boost control worth fifteen decibels either way. Highs run from one point six to twenty-five kilohertz, shelving or peaking with a Q from two to ten; mids from zero point four to six point four kilohertz, always peaking; lows from twenty-five to four hundred hertz. A make-before-break insert button keeps switching free of clicks, and a current-limited supply filter isolates it from the power rails. The archive lists the M462 at CFTO, Maryland, TVO and the Toronto Transit Commission.

2.12 Inside the M490F

VO As radio went stereo, a single fader had to carry two matched channels. The M490F stereo input module selects one of two stereo sources and delivers them to the program mixing buses. Each side has a balanced insert for an equalizer or compressor, then a preamplifier presenting about thirty thousand ohms, with twenty decibels of sensitivity range. One stereo attenuator handles both sides. A CMOS analog switch turns the channel on, and off with more than one hundred decibels of attenuation. A balance control raises one side while lowering the other, keeping total power essentially constant, and cue feeds bridge the attenuator input without disturbing the program. A CMOS flip-flop latches channel on and off, takes remote commands, and closes contacts to start and stop tape machines. The archive lists the M490F in R-series radio console literature.

2.14 The 500 series came and went

VO In the eighties the 500 series takes over the stereo consoles: the M520 and M521 inputs, the M523 submaster, the M562C stereo EQ, and the M524A, a programming computer in a module. 2.15 The technology: microprocessors move into the console

VO In the eighties the computer moves into the console. MicroCOM used microprocessor-controlled crosspoints that could be reprogrammed show by show, at ABC Hollywood, the CBC, in England and at WLS. MicroCOM II added PC software for building matrix, terminal, group and IFB files and loading them into the system. The ST console offered a 4-band EQ, an integral routing switcher, a central microprocessor controller, a programming keyboard, alphanumeric displays, a peak annunciator and an audio-follow-video interface, in 24-, 36- or 48-channel frames. The M524A put the programming computer and its display screens in a module. Ward-Beck's own LS series brochure calls it a 'fourth generation console system', built for stereo TV, ENG sources, one-inch tape and the mix-minus feeds needed for IFB to talent and talk-show callers. On the faders, the L-series drawings specify Penny & Giles.

2.16 Inside the M5200

VO By the mid-eighties, a Ward-Beck console had a brain. The M5200 controller is the central, microprocessor-based unit of the ST series of stereo television consoles, supervising channel muting, the monitoring channels and optional programmers, overload displays and routing switchers. At its heart is an N S C eight hundred CMOS microprocessor running the Z eighty instruction set, with its operating system in programmable read-only memory and the mute and machine control codes kept in electrically erasable memory. A comparator senses a sagging supply and halts the processor before power is lost; a battery-backed timer then decides whether a brief interruption should restore the console exactly as it was, or a long outage should leave everything safely shut down. A hardware watchdog resets the processor if the software ever goes astray. For the operator, the console could now remember itself.
