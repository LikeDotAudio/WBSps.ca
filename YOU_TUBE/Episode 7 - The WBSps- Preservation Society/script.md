# Episode 7: The WBSps: Preservation Society (2003|36 – 2026|59)

**Runtime target:** 10 min · **Voice-over:** ~1908 words · **Slides:** 109

> In 2003 a twenty-year-old in Saskatoon moved a Ward-Beck console out of CBC Regina and started a website to find its manuals. Twenty-three years later that site is WBSps.ca and WBSps.Kuzub.com: a sorted archive of almost 15,000 files. The story of the Ward-Beck Systems Preservation Society, and of what people did after the consoles.

## Script and slides

### 7.1 Cold open: it rained on the way to Regina

**VO:** May 2003. A console is coming out of Studio 2 at the CBC in Regina. It was installed in September 1982 and cost 137 thousand dollars. Twenty M470A preamps, nineteen M462B equalizers, eight M466 compressors, a 384-point patch bay. Every module is pulled and packed. The frame goes on Kevin's truck. The truck box is eight feet long, and the console is six foot eight. It rains on the way, so it rides in poly under a tarp. It fits in the elevator, just. The room it's going to is a hundred inches wide. It fits with twenty inches to spare.

### 7.2 A kid with a console

**VO:** The new owner was Tony Kuzub, Anthony Peter Kuzub, who ran a recording studio called High Voltage Recording. [ARCHIVIST: confirm the studio was in Saskatoon; the 2009 letter is addressed there.] He wrote on the site that he was a twenty-year-old kid. A console that size doesn't come with its paperwork. He needed the manuals to rewire it for his multitrack, so he started asking around. Then he put what he found on a website, under a name with a capital S at the end: the Ward-Beck Systems Preservation Society. He called himself its president. Its goal, in the site's own words: to keep vintage Ward-Beck equipment running and working by supplying a database of documentation, knowledge and support.

### 7.3 August 2003: the box from Toronto

**VO:** The first news entry is August 20, 2003. Eugene at Ward-Beck had written to say he'd look through the company's files. Nine days later a box arrived. 'Let's just say ten pounds of Ward-Beck manuals is a lot.' Monitor modules, meters, submasters, distribution amplifiers, power supplies: more than sixty titles. Inside was a gift, a prototype POD22 with glowing LED meters. [ARCHIVIST: the archive dates the POD22 to 2014; the 2003 news calls it a prototype.] Then the scanning started, one manual at a time, at night, between studio bookings. The M466 compressor manual alone was fifty pages. Each one went up as a PDF.

### 7.4 The engineer who kept the files

**VO:** Eugene is Eugene L. Johnson. His initials, ELJ, are on eighty Ward-Beck drawings from 1976 to 1985, as draftsman, checker and engineer. By the time the society found him, he was the company's Managing Director. He's the reason so much of this archive exists at all. He sent manuals, pictures and documents, and the old site's thank-you list says it plainly: 'Eugene J. - MANY.' The man who once signed off the M470E functional diagram became the man who handed its manual to a stranger in Saskatchewan.

### 7.6 Dr. Beck

**VO:** One member got a nickname. Dave Thomas, in Vancouver, became 'Dr. Beck', because he knew too much and had a prescription for everything. He'd taken apart a 32 by 16 console from a CBC television studio in Vancouver. The frame was too big to keep. So he cleaned the pots, replaced the switches, and built cases that held two modules each, with power supplies and phantom power. He widened the equalizer curves on M460 and M462 modules. His notes went on the maintenance page. Clean Penny and Giles faders gently, and never touch the cat's whisker wiper contacts.

### 7.7 Racked, parted out, rescued

**VO:** That was the pattern after the consoles left the stations. A Ward-Beck desk can weigh over 650 pounds. Most were built for news and network TV, not stereo music. So many were parted out, and the modules were racked and sold one or two at a time. The site kept a rack-mounting page of good examples and bad ones, and a price guide of what modules were fetching. Tony also became what he called a protector. In 2004 a buyer in Nashville won a 48-channel console on eBay to track with. Tony warned him it had only six microphone preamps.

### 7.11 Back at Ward-Beck

**VO:** Then the story turned around. On April 8, 2013, the company's job binders were photographed on the shelf, spine by spine. There are 535 of those pictures now, and each spine names a job. Drawings came out under a desk lamp to be photographed. ### 7.15 The module arranger: RTO-144

**VO:** A rack for vintage modules has one hard problem. Every Ward-Beck card keeps its signals on different pins of the same card edge: twenty-two contacts on top, A to Z underneath. The RTO-144 is the module arranger. Issue 1 puts the card edge across the middle of the board and a QTP connector at each end, with a block marked old and new and a switched 48-volt phantom feed. A jumper calculator sets it for each module. For Ward-Beck's fiftieth anniversary the RTO was offered through QuadTwistedPair.com. [ARCHIVIST: confirm the RTO and the fiftieth anniversary.] Two M460A input modules, racked side by side, in 2020.
